Strategic Waste Framework: Bangkok 2051

Intercepting urban waste to restore natural habitats, rebuild aquatic biodiversity, and secure access to affordable, nutritious food.

Current Crisis 2050 Forecast Doughnut Framework Proposed Solutions Sources

The Current State of Bangkok

Bangkok generates over 4 million tonnes of waste annually. Beyond the methane emitted from landfills, one of the most critical failures of our current system is ecological leakage. Plastic pollution and toxic runoff are actively destroying aquatic ecosystems, threatening local wildlife, and eventually reaching the ocean via the Chao Phraya River.

Ecological Leakage

Thailand is identified globally as a major contributor to marine plastic. Plastic suffocates Bangkok's waterways, directly killing fish populations and destroying the habitats necessary for biodiversity to thrive.

Food Chain Contamination

As plastics break down in the khlongs and the Chao Phraya, microplastics threaten aquatic life. High volumes of organic waste and mixed garbage increase costs and make sustainable waste management difficult.

Social Gaps

Thai people are not educated on what recycling is and how to recycle. Government directions are not being properly followed by companies, and most separated bins can not be found in the streets.

2050 Predictive Modeling

Mathematical forecasting models predict a severe escalation of Bangkok's waste crisis if we maintain current habits.

Business-as-Usual Trajectory

  • Volume Increase: Municipal solid waste is projected to grow at 1.15% annually, reaching 39.61 million tons by 2050.
  • Habitat Destruction: Global plastic generation is expected to rise from 380 million tons in 2022 to roughly 590 million tons by 2050.
  • Emission Surge: Urban waste emissions in Thailand are projected to be 19.9 million tons of CO2 equivalent over the next 25 years.

The Mitigation Target

By transitioning from open landfills to strict mandatory source-separation of organic and biodegradable waste, models show Bangkok can bring waste emissions down to 4.6 million tons.

The Doughnut Economic Framework

Our physical solution is engineered specifically to operate within the Doughnut Model, bridging the gap between ecological preservation and human needs.

Ecological Ceiling: Natural Habitats & Biodiversity

Poor waste management damages habitats through landfill expansion, plastic pollution, and contaminated waterways. Our model decreases pollution, protects natural habitats, and reduces harm to biodiversity by preventing plastics from entering ecosystems where they destroy wildlife.

Social Foundation: Access to Nutritious Food

Through our model, edible surplus food from households, supermarkets, and restaurants is redistributed to communities in need, improving access to affordable and nutritious food rather than being discarded.

To create a sustainable waste-to-resource circular economy, Team 24 proposes creating Circular Food & Waste Hubs in Bangkok that collect waste directly from markets and households.

⚠️ System Downside & Maintenance Constraints

The primary downside is odor and pest management if the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio is not perfectly maintained by the community in the composting sector. Furthermore, it requires strict behavioral compliance; if non-organic waste contaminates the composting chamber, the resulting fertilizer will be unusable for food crops.

Solution 2: Plastic to Urban Farming Infrastructure

To aggressively tackle Bangkok's landfill dependency and prevent ecological leakage, we propose a closed-loop dry waste recycling system that directly supports local food growth.

What It Does

This infrastructure physically prevents plastic from reaching open landfills or waterways. By sorting recyclables at the source, the hub recycles plastic into urban farming infrastructure. This drastically reduces the total mass sent to landfills and protects local habitats.

How It Works

The system utilizes standardized drop-off points. Once materials are collected, recycled plastics are reformed into structural elements for community gardens and rooftop farms. By pairing this with the compost generated in Solution 1, the infrastructure creates a closed loop used to grow affordable, nutritious food directly in the city.

Economic Impact: Internalizing the Cost of Plastic

Understanding the Negative Externality: A negative production externality occurs when the disposal of a good (like single-use plastic) creates indirect costs to society—such as habitat destruction and microplastic pollution in our waterways—that the waste generator does not pay for. Because the free market only considers the private costs of disposal (the Marginal Private Cost, or MPC), it overproduces plastic waste at an inefficiently high quantity (Qm).

The Solution: Our Closed-Loop Urban Farming Hub acts as a mechanism to internalize this external cost. By physically capturing the waste and assigning it immediate value as agricultural infrastructure, the system shifts the market away from the overproduction of waste to the socially optimal point (Qopt). This shift effectively eliminates the deadweight loss (the red shaded triangle), saving Bangkok's aquatic biodiversity from destruction.

⚠️ System Downside & Maintenance Constraints

The major constraint is the initial manufacturing cost of the sorting and processing units. Additionally, the system relies heavily on users pre-washing their plastics; heavily soiled containers can contaminate the batch, attract pests, and halt the production of the urban farming infrastructure.
